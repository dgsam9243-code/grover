# Grover user testing plan

**Goal:** find out whether Grover actually helps neurodivergent people in busy social settings, and what to change. Do this *with* participants, not *to* them.

**Size:** 5–8 participants per round. Small qualitative rounds find most usability problems; repeat after changes.

---

## 1. Who to invite

- Adults (18+) for the first round. Include teens only with guardian consent plus the teen's own agreement, and follow any school or ethics rules that apply.
- A mix across the support profiles: Social clarity, Focus, Sensory comfort, Listening support.
- Recruit through neurodivergent-led groups and communities, and **pay participants** for their time.
- If this runs through a university or a clinic, get **ethics (IRB) approval first**.

## 2. Make the session accessible

Ask everyone beforehand what helps them, and offer these by default:

- A quiet room, dimmable lights, no surprise noises, and a clear way to leave.
- An **agenda sent in advance** (what will happen, how long, who is there), with no surprise tasks.
- **Breaks whenever asked.** A visible "break" card on the table they can point to.
- Answer in their preferred way: speaking, typing, pointing to a scale, or yes/no cards.
- Plain-language consent (below). No deception, and no tasks secretly designed to cause stress.
- A support person can join if the participant wants.
- Sessions last no more than 45 minutes.

## 3. Consent (plain language)

> We are testing an app called Grover. We want to know if it is useful and easy, not test you.
> You can stop at any time, without giving a reason. You still get paid.
> We will write notes. We will only record audio or screen if you say yes. You can say no.
> Your name will not be in our reports.
> Grover is not a medical tool. It can make mistakes.

Record each choice separately: notes ✓, audio recording yes/no, screen recording yes/no, contact for future rounds yes/no.

## 4. Tasks (lab session)

Use the **demo scenario** so everyone sees the same scene, then a short live try in a mildly busy room if the participant wants to.

| # | Task | What it tests | Rubric |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | First open: pick the profile that fits you | Is the welcome clear, and do people recognise themselves in a profile? | R3, R10 |
| T2 | Watch the demo. When the card changes, say what it means | Can a cue be understood **at a glance**? | R1, R2 |
| T3 | Find the words Grover suggests saying | Is "You could say" clear and usable? | R6 |
| T4 | You feel overwhelmed. Get a moment to calm down | Is "I need a moment" easy to find and use? Does it help? | R8 |
| T5 | Make it so others can't tell you're using it | Is discreet mode findable and good enough? | R5 |
| T6 | Turn off one kind of alert you don't want | Are Settings understandable? | R10, R9 |
| T7 | (Optional) Share your setup with someone | Is the setup code understandable? | Phase 2 |

## 5. What to measure

Keep measures light. **Comfort comes first: skip anything a participant doesn't want to do.**

- **Task success:** done alone / done with a hint / not done.
- **Glance time (T2):** seconds from a card appearing until the participant says what it means.
- **Understanding (T2):** did they read the cue the way it was meant? Note any **literal misreadings**. These feed R1.
- **False or annoying cues:** "Did anything show up that was wrong or not needed?" (R9)
- **Calm rating:** before, during and after the demo, on a 1–5 visual scale (faces or colours). The goal is not a rise in stress.
- **Ease of use:** the System Usability Scale (10 items), with plain wording and read aloud if wanted. Note any wording changes in the report.
- **Would you use it?** "Where would you use this? Where would you not?"

## 6. Interview (5–10 minutes, optional)

1. What was most useful?
2. What was confusing, too much, or annoying?
3. Did anything make you feel judged or "corrected"? (R4: this must be zero.)
4. Would people around you notice? Would that matter to you? (R5)
5. If you could change one thing, what would it be?
6. Which profile felt most like you? What is missing?

## 7. Optional field try (1 week)

For participants who want it: use Grover in real life for a week (installed PWA, earbuds, their chosen profile).

- After each use, a 2-question check-in: "Did it help? (yes / a bit / no)" and "Anything wrong?"
- At the end, a 15-minute follow-up call.
- No data leaves the phone. Participants report only what they choose to share; the session summary helps them remember.

## 8. Co-design

- Invite 2–3 participants to stay on as **paid advisors** for later rounds.
- Show them what changed because of their feedback.
- Ask them to help rewrite cue wording and profile descriptions in their own words.

## 9. Analysis and next steps

- Put every problem in a table: what happened, how many people, severity (blocks / slows / annoys), and rubric criterion.
- Fix the problems that block or slow people first, then re-test with new participants.
- Update the rubric scores in `ITERATIONS.md` using **observed** evidence instead of the developer's estimate. Scores from real users override earlier self-scores.

## Success looks like

- Most participants understand a cue in **under 3 seconds** and read it correctly.
- **No one** reports feeling judged or corrected.
- Calm ratings stay the same or improve during the demo.
- At least half name a real place they would use Grover.
